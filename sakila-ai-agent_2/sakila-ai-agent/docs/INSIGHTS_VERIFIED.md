# The six insights, verified against the Sakila database

_Generated by `scripts/generate_insights_doc.py`. Data: 41 recorded rental dates in 6 bursts; 174-day gap (2005-08-23 to 2006-02-14); no rentals in 2005-09, 2005-10, 2005-11, 2005-12, 2006-01._

## Insight 1: Rental-duration policy does not match actual holding time
**Business question:** Does the contractual rental duration (3-7 days) match how long customers actually keep DVDs?

**Verified findings**
- 15,861 completed rentals (all data; 183 never returned) are kept 5.03 days on average.
- Average holding time is ~5 days for every policy: 3-day 5.02, 4-day 5.06, 5-day 4.98, 6-day 5.03, 7-day 5.04.
- Holding time is statistically unrelated to the policy (correlation -0.0006).
- Late share (exact time) falls from 72.07% (3-day) to 29.06% (7-day); using calendar days it is 66.87% to 23.34%.
- Holding days are spread almost UNIFORMLY from 1 to 9 days (each ~11% of rentals); only ~33% are returned on day 4-6. The ~5-day average is the middle of a flat distribution, not a peak.
- Late copy-days beyond contract: 20,192; unused allowance days: 18,795 (calendar-day basis).

**Check of the written report**
- Report's late rates (72.07% for 3-day ... 29.06% for 7-day) are reproduced with the exact-time definition; they are lower with calendar days. Always state the definition.
- Report says customers have a 'stable ~5-day rental horizon'. The AVERAGE is ~5 for every policy, but the distribution is flat (1-9 days), so '5 days' is not a typical behaviour; the policy mainly decides who is labelled late.
- Why customers return late (e.g. treating late fees as an extension fee) is not observable; it is a hypothesis.

**Metrics to watch**
- `avg_actual_hold_days`: mean (return_date - rental_date) in days
- `pct_late`: share of rentals kept longer than rental_duration (state exact-time or calendar-day definition)
- `late_copy_days`: sum of days beyond contract = inventory time locked by late rentals
- `unused_allowance_days`: contract days not used by early returns
- `pct_returned_day_4_to_6`: concentration around 5 days (tests the '5-day horizon' claim)

**Caveats**
- A customer is 'late' because the policy threshold is exceeded: the same holding time is late under 3 days and early under 7 days (policy exposure, not worse behaviour).
- Changing all durations to 5 days would change the labels, but the effect on real returns is a counterfactual that the data cannot show (Lucas critique: behaviour may change under a new policy).
- Lost revenue from late returns is not observed (no record of customers who could not rent).

**Recommendations (hypotheses to test)**
- Test (A/B, one store or a subset of titles) a pre-due-date extension option priced below the late fee, and measure late_copy_days and availability.
- Track actual holding time alongside the contract, by title demand level.

**Follow-up SQL**
```sql
SELECT f.rental_duration,
       COUNT(*) AS completed_rentals,
       AVG((julianday(r.return_date) - julianday(r.rental_date))) AS avg_hold_days,   -- MySQL: TIMESTAMPDIFF(SECOND, r.rental_date, r.return_date)/86400
       100.0 * SUM(CASE WHEN julianday(r.return_date) - julianday(r.rental_date) > f.rental_duration THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) / COUNT(*) AS pct_late
FROM rental r JOIN inventory i ON i.inventory_id = r.inventory_id JOIN film f ON f.film_id = i.film_id
WHERE r.return_date IS NOT NULL
GROUP BY f.rental_duration ORDER BY f.rental_duration;
```

## Insight 2: Customer loyalty (returning) does not translate into category loyalty
**Business question:** When customers come back, do they stay in the same category?

**Verified findings**
- All 599 customers return (min 12, median 26 rentals).
- 93.19% of 15,263 consecutive rentals switch category; 6.81% repeat.
- Rental 2 repeats rental 1's category in 7.51% of customers; of the 45 customers whose first two rentals share a category, 3 continue on rental 3.
- Median customer has rented 13 of 16 categories; 98% have rented 10+.
- Chance benchmark: random choice gives 6.32% repeats; shuffling each customer's own history gives 6.39% (permutation p = 0.0166). Repetition is only slightly above chance.

**Check of the written report**
- 'Customers want exploration' is an interpretation: the switching rate is close to what random choice among 16 evenly-demanded categories produces.
- Loyalty must be defined: store loyalty (returning) is 100%; category loyalty (repeat) is low.

**Metrics to watch**
- `repeat_pct`: consecutive rentals in the same category
- `switch_pct`: consecutive rentals changing category
- `distinct_categories_per_customer`: category diversity
- `random_choice_repeat_pct`: chance benchmark

**Caveats**
- Low repetition does not mean categories have no value; it means the previous category is a weak predictor.
- Same-timestamp rentals are ordered by rental_id (tie-breaker).

**Recommendations (hypotheses to test)**
- Promote discovery across categories rather than 'more of the same' genre.
- Define loyalty KPIs separately: store retention vs category affinity.

**Follow-up SQL**
```sql
WITH seq AS (
  SELECT r.customer_id, c.name AS category,
         LAG(c.name) OVER (PARTITION BY r.customer_id ORDER BY r.rental_date, r.rental_id) AS prev_category
  FROM rental r JOIN inventory i ON i.inventory_id = r.inventory_id
  JOIN film_category fc ON fc.film_id = i.film_id JOIN category c ON c.category_id = fc.category_id
  WHERE r.rental_date < '2006-01-01')
SELECT 100.0 * SUM(CASE WHEN category = prev_category THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) / COUNT(*) AS repeat_pct
FROM seq WHERE prev_category IS NOT NULL;
```

## Insight 3: Customers rent while still holding DVDs; zero holdings are a repeated pause, not churn
**Business question:** How many DVDs do customers hold when they rent again, and how quickly do they come back after returning everything?

**Verified findings**
- At 15,862 checkouts (window) customers already hold 2.44 DVDs on average; 79.77% of checkouts happen while holding at least one (0: 20.23%, 1: 19.82%, 2: 17.66%, 3+: 42.29%).
- 3,208 zero-holding episodes across 599 customers; 2,610 are followed by another rental inside the window and 598 are censored (no later data), not churn.
- Calendar clock: 67.93% of reactivations happen 7-14 days later.
- Recorded-date clock (counting only dates with any rental): 11.99% by the 1st, 61.84% by the 2nd, 81.84% by the 3rd, 91.26% by the 4th recorded date.
- The two clocks classify 248 episodes (213 customers) differently (early = within 4 recorded dates vs within 14 calendar days).

**Check of the written report**
- Report's 2.44 average, 3,208 episodes and 67.93% (7-14 days) are reproduced.
- The 7-14 day 'wait' is largely an artefact of the data: rentals were recorded in 8-day bursts separated by ~2-week gaps, so a return near the end of a burst can only be followed by a rental in the next burst.
- Holding 3 DVDs does not CAUSE more visits; the association is observational. The mean (2.44) is not an optimal target.

**Metrics to watch**
- `avg_prior_open_rentals`: DVDs already held at checkout (interval reconstruction)
- `zero_state_episodes`: returns that leave the customer holding nothing
- `reactivation_by_recorded_date`: share of reactivations within k recorded dates
- `censored_episodes`: zero states with no later observation (not churn)

**Caveats**
- Zero holdings is not churn; churn cannot be observed in a finite, censored dataset.
- Episodes are not unique customers (a customer can have many episodes).
- The 174-day gap and 2-week gaps between bursts distort calendar-day measures.

**Recommendations (hypotheses to test)**
- When a customer is down to 1 open DVD, show 2-3 next-title suggestions at return; test with a control group.

**Follow-up SQL**
```sql
-- DVDs held at each checkout (interval reconstruction)
SELECT cur.rental_id, COUNT(prev.rental_id) AS prior_open_rentals
FROM rental cur
LEFT JOIN rental prev ON prev.customer_id = cur.customer_id AND prev.rental_id <> cur.rental_id
     AND prev.rental_date < cur.rental_date AND (prev.return_date IS NULL OR prev.return_date > cur.rental_date)
WHERE cur.rental_date < '2006-01-01'
GROUP BY cur.rental_id;
```

## Insight 4: Category transitions: the strongest routes are weak signals
**Business question:** After category A, is category B disproportionately likely next (lift), and is it usable for bundles/recommendations?

**Verified findings**
- Top lifts (all next rentals as denominator): Classics->Comedy lift 1.32 (70 transitions, z 2.46); Games->Action lift 1.30 (83 transitions, z 2.55); Family->Travel lift 1.29 (70 transitions, z 2.27); New->Classics lift 1.28 (67 transitions, z 2.13); Children->Sports lift 1.25 (83 transitions, z 2.20).
- Report's definition (switches only as denominator) gives: Games->Action 1.41; Classics->Comedy 1.40; Family->Travel 1.37; New->Classics 1.37; Children->Sports 1.34. Same pairs, higher values because repeats are removed from the denominator.
- Whole matrix: chi-square p = 0.4351, Cramer's V = 0.0315; 15 of 256 pairs exceed |z| 1.96 (~13 expected by chance); 0 survive Bonferroni.
- Hold-out test: transition-based top-3 hit rate 19.87% vs random 18.75%.

**Check of the written report**
- Games->Action lift ~1.4 is reproduced under the report's definition, but it is not statistically distinguishable from chance after multiple-comparison correction.
- Lift 1.4 means B is 1.4 times as likely as its baseline share, NOT that 140% of customers rent B.
- Lift is association, not causation (A does not make customers want B).
- Each Sakila film has exactly one category (film_category is one-to-one here).

**Metrics to watch**
- `transition_probability`: P(B|A) = count(A->B)/count(A->any)
- `lift`: P(B|A) / share of B (controls for B's popularity)
- `z_residual`: deviation from independence; judge against multiple comparisons
- `hit@3`: hold-out predictive accuracy

**Caveats**
- Use lift, not raw counts (raw counts favour big categories).
- With 256 pairs some high lifts appear by chance.

**Recommendations (hypotheses to test)**
- Treat high-lift pairs as candidates for a small bundle/placement test, not as proven demand routes.

**Follow-up SQL**
```sql
WITH seq AS (
  SELECT r.customer_id, c.name AS category,
         LAG(c.name) OVER (PARTITION BY r.customer_id ORDER BY r.rental_date, r.rental_id) AS prev_category
  FROM rental r JOIN inventory i ON i.inventory_id = r.inventory_id
  JOIN film_category fc ON fc.film_id = i.film_id JOIN category c ON c.category_id = fc.category_id
  WHERE r.rental_date < '2006-01-01'),
pairs AS (SELECT prev_category AS a, category AS b, COUNT(*) AS n FROM seq WHERE prev_category IS NOT NULL GROUP BY prev_category, category),
out_a AS (SELECT a, SUM(n) AS n_a FROM pairs GROUP BY a),
share_b AS (SELECT b, 1.0 * SUM(n) / (SELECT SUM(n) FROM pairs) AS share FROM pairs GROUP BY b)
SELECT p.a AS from_category, p.b AS to_category, p.n AS transitions,
       1.0 * p.n / o.n_a AS p_b_given_a, (1.0 * p.n / o.n_a) / s.share AS lift
FROM pairs p JOIN out_a o ON o.a = p.a JOIN share_b s ON s.b = p.b
WHERE p.a <> p.b
ORDER BY lift DESC;
```

## Insight 5: Every customer is a hybrid buyer; demand per title is similar across price tiers
**Business question:** Are $0.99 renters bargain hunters, and is there room to bundle single $0.99 'visits' into bigger baskets?

**Verified findings**
- Customer segments by price tiers used: {'hybrid': 599} (all customers mix $0.99 with $2.99/$4.99 titles).
- Same-day 'visits' containing a $0.99 title: 4,876; 51.33% are a single $0.99 disc; 2-disc visits average $4.36 list value.
- Rentals per title by price: $0.99: 17.34, $2.99: 16.41, $4.99: 16.48 (nearly equal).

**Check of the written report**
- A 'visit' is approximated as customer + calendar date; Sakila has no visit/basket id, so basket figures are a heuristic.
- Report text says 48.67% of visits have 'three or more' rentals; it is two or more.
- Equal demand across fixed price tiers is NOT a price-elasticity estimate: each title has one fixed price, and price is confounded with the title.
- The $250 bundle upside in the report is an assumption-based illustration, not a forecast.

**Metrics to watch**
- `single_0.99_visit_share`: same-day visits with only one $0.99 disc
- `avg_visit_value`: sum of rental_rate per same-day visit
- `rentals_per_title_by_tier`: demand per title at each fixed price

**Caveats**
- Lucas critique: behaviour observed under today's fixed prices and no bundles may change once bundles/combos exist, so historical ratios cannot predict the effect of a new pricing policy.
- No cost data: profit/margin of a bundle cannot be computed; replacement_cost is not acquisition cost.

**Recommendations (hypotheses to test)**
- Pilot a combo (e.g. 2 same-category $0.99 titles priced between $0.99 and $1.98, the report's example is $1.49) in one store or period; compare basket size, revenue per visit and availability with a control group before rollout.

**Follow-up SQL**
```sql
SELECT customer_id, DATE(rental_date) AS visit_day, COUNT(*) AS discs,
       SUM(f.rental_rate) AS visit_value
FROM rental r JOIN inventory i ON i.inventory_id = r.inventory_id JOIN film f ON f.film_id = i.film_id
GROUP BY customer_id, DATE(rental_date);
```

## Insight 6: Rentals track the number of copies; productivity differs by film
**Business question:** Do films with more copies get more rentals, and are copies used equally productively?

**Verified findings**
- 958 of 1000 films have copies (42 have none, so their zero rentals mean 'not stocked', not 'unpopular').
- Spearman correlation copies vs rentals: rho = 0.935 (p < 0.001) - strong association.
- Productivity quartiles (rentals per copy): Q1: 2.85 (1107 copies, 3150 rentals); Q2: 3.34 (1197 copies, 3993 rentals); Q3: 3.68 (1239 copies, 4557 rentals); Q4: 4.18 (1038 copies, 4344 rentals).
- Average rentals per copy barely changes with the number of copies: 2 copies 3.60, 3 copies 3.54, 4 copies 3.54, 5 copies 3.53, 6 copies 3.46, 7 copies 3.48, 8 copies 3.47 - rentals rise almost proportionally with copies.
- Rentals per copy by category range from 3.41 (Horror) to 3.58 (Music).

**Check of the written report**
- Report's rho 0.935 is reproduced; quartile totals differ slightly because of tie handling.
- Association, not causation: films with more demand may have been given more copies; adding copies is not proven to add rentals.
- Rentals per copy is a utilisation proxy; unmet demand, costs and profit are not in the data, so 'which category to buy more of' cannot be concluded from it alone.

**Metrics to watch**
- `rentals_per_copy`: rentals / physical copies (utilisation proxy)
- `films_with_no_copies`: catalogue titles that cannot be rented
- `spearman_rho`: rank association copies vs rentals

**Caveats**
- A film with zero rentals may simply have no copies or short observation.
- Removal/purchase decisions need costs and unmet demand, which are not recorded.

**Recommendations (hypotheses to test)**
- Review high-productivity/low-copy films for a small copy increase and measure the change; review low-productivity/high-copy films before any reduction.

**Follow-up SQL**
```sql
SELECT f.film_id, f.title, COUNT(DISTINCT i.inventory_id) AS copies, COUNT(r.rental_id) AS rentals,
       1.0 * COUNT(r.rental_id) / NULLIF(COUNT(DISTINCT i.inventory_id), 0) AS rentals_per_copy
FROM film f LEFT JOIN inventory i ON i.film_id = f.film_id LEFT JOIN rental r ON r.inventory_id = i.inventory_id
GROUP BY f.film_id, f.title ORDER BY rentals_per_copy DESC;
```

## What the data cannot show

- **chronology**: Rentals exist only on 41 dates: five 8-day bursts in May-Aug 2005 plus one day (2006-02-14). There is a 174-day gap (Aug 24 2005 - Feb 13 2006); Sep 2005-Jan 2006 have no data. Month/quarter/year growth, seasonality, churn by month and forecasts are unreliable; Feb 14 cannot be attributed to Valentine's Day.
- **visits**: No visit, basket, session, browsing, search, store-entry or display data. Same customer + same date is only a heuristic for a visit.
- **demographics**: No age, gender, income, wealth, tourist status or acquisition channel. Customer address is not a demand location. No demographic personas or LTV by demographic.
- **causality**: All data is observational: no experiments. Correlations (price, length, copies, lift, staff, holdings) cannot prove causes; counterfactuals (price cuts, policy changes, more copies) cannot be measured. Lucas critique: behaviour under current policies may change when policies change.
- **unobserved_demand**: Failed rentals, unavailable-copy events, substitutions, abandonment and unmet demand are not recorded, so lost sales and lost revenue cannot be measured.
- **money**: No acquisition cost, operating cost, commissions or profit. replacement_cost is a replacement charge attribute, not purchase cost. payment rows have no charge-type field; amount - rental_rate is only a late-fee proxy (matches days late exactly in ~72% of rentals, and 852 late rentals have no extra charge).
- **ratings**: film.rating is an MPAA content classification (G/PG/PG-13/R/NC-17), not a customer review score. No satisfaction or service-quality data.
- **staff_store**: Two stores and two staff. A rental can be linked to the customer's store, the inventory's store or the staff member's store; state which. Store 'efficiency', market share, closures and new locations need data Sakila does not have.
- **actors**: A film has several actors; attributing a film's full revenue to each actor double-counts. Renting a film with an actor does not prove preference for that actor.
- **churn**: Churn and lifetime value cannot be observed in a short, censored window; zero holdings is not churn.
- **forecast**: No reliable demand forecast: 4 months of bursty, partly synthetic data with a large gap. Offer scenario methods and what data is needed, not point forecasts.
- **bcg**: A true BCG matrix needs market growth and relative market share against competitors; Sakila has neither. Only an internal proxy (share of Sakila rentals vs change between bursts) is possible, and it must not justify betting on one category: demand is spread evenly and customers switch 93% of the time.
